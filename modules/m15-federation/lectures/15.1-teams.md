# 15.1 One graph, many teams

## Hook (2 min)

Shelfie's schema has 60-odd fields and one team. Now four teams want to ship on their own schedule. Split the
service and the clients get four APIs, or a BFF team becomes the queue every feature waits in. Federation's
promise: four teams, one graph, no queue. Its price is paid in the map of who owns what.

## Concept (15 min)

### Three shapes

- **Monolith**: one schema, one deploy. Cheapest per feature while one team can hold it; every feature rides the
  same release train.
- **BFF over services**: services own data, a BFF team owns the API. Each cross-service feature is a handover:
  service PR, BFF PR, deploy in order.
- **Federated graph**: each team owns a **subgraph**, a GraphQL service with its part of the schema; a
  **router** plans each query across them; **composition** checks, before deploy, that the parts fit.

### Netflix

InfoQ, "Evolving the Federated GraphQL Platform at Netflix" (Kavitha Srinivasan, 2023-08-14): their federated
graph had "more than 200 services" at the time of writing, and the platform's way to build them is the DGS
Framework (Spring Boot + graphql-java), open source. The course's reading: the article spends its pages on
schema development and tooling, not on the router; at that size, governance is the work.

### Drawing the map

- A field belongs to the team whose **data** computes it, not to the type it sits on: `Book.averageRating` is
  reviews'.
- A type that another team points at, or adds fields to, becomes an **entity** and needs a **key**
  (`User @key(fields: "id")`). Keys are the only coupling teams agree on.
- Conway's law runs both ways: the graph will follow the org chart. Split where teams are, not where types are.
- Not everything needs federation. Two teams, one release cadence: a modular monolith is cheaper.

## Experiment (15 min)

Estimate "club book of the month" (clubs, catalog, reviews and accounts all touched) under the three shapes: PRs,
contracts, meetings, deploy order, who waits. Mini-project 15.8 builds it for real; compare your estimate there.

## Check

1. Why is `Book.reviews` owned by reviews and not catalog?
2. Which types need a key, and why not every type?
3. When is federation the wrong answer?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Its data lives in reviews; catalog would have to call reviews to answer it, putting a service dependency where
   the router should do the join.
2. Types another team references or extends: a key is how the router fetches "that one" from its owner. A type only
   its owner touches needs none.
3. When one team (or a few on one cadence) owns the graph: the router, composition and per-subgraph deploys cost
   more than the coordination they save.

# 8.1 Offset pagination and its limits

## Hook (2 min)

`books(page: 2, size: 20)` is one line with Spring Data. Now someone posts three books while you read
page 0, and page 1 opens with three books you just saw. Or someone opens page 49,999 of a million rows.
Both failures come from what `OFFSET` means.

## Concept (15 min)

### What OFFSET means

`LIMIT 20 OFFSET 40` says "skip the first 40 rows of the list *as it is now*, return the next 20". It
does not mean "continue after the book I saw last". Two consequences:

- **Drift.** An insert above the window pushes every row down: the next page repeats rows.
  A delete above it pulls rows up: the next page skips rows. Lab 8.1 measures exactly 12 duplicates
  (3 inserts after each of 4 pages) and exactly 12 gaps (3 deletes per page). Users see the duplicates,
  and nobody notices the gaps.
- **Deep-offset cost.** The database still has to produce the skipped rows in order. With an index it
  walks the index and throws them away: 20 rows at offset 900,000 of 1,000,000 read 900,020 rows
  (53 ms; offset 0: 0.02 ms) on PostgreSQL 18.7 (`_workbench/2026-11-21-m08-probe/deep.txt`).

### The count nobody asked for

Spring Data's `Page<T>` runs a second statement for `totalElements`: `select count(...)` over every
match, on every request. Spring skips it only when a page comes back shorter than its size. At a million rows that count is about 23 ms by itself, most of what
page 0 costs. `Slice<T>` fetches `size + 1` rows instead and only knows whether there is a next page.

### When offset is right

Jump-to-page UIs, admin tables, search results nobody reads past page 10, data that rarely changes.
Offsets are simple and cacheable by URL. Feeds that grow at the top, and long lists, need cursors (8.2).

## Java build (20 min)

Lab 8.1: `books(page, size)` with `findAll(PageRequest.of(page, size, sort))` on PostgreSQL, a total
order (`addedAt desc, id desc`), `BAD_REQUEST` for a negative page or a size outside 1 to 50, and
`addBook`/`deleteBook` mutations for the experiment.

## Experiment (15 min)

`Drift.java` scrolls 5 pages while someone inserts, then deletes, 3 books per page. Then load a million
rows and time pages 0, 500, 5,000 and 49,999. Reference run: about 20 ms, 20 ms, 25 ms and 102 ms per
request.

## Check (3 min)

1. Why does a delete cause gaps and not duplicates?
2. A sort by `addedAt` alone looked fine in tests. What goes wrong in production?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Rows below the deleted one move up one position. The row that moves into the window you already
   read is never shown. Nothing moves down, so nothing repeats.
2. Ties. Two books with the same `addedAt` may come back in either order, and in a different order on the next
   request, so one can appear on two pages and another on none. Always end the sort with a unique
   key (`id`).
</details>
